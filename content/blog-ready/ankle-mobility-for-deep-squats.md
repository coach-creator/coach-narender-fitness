If your heels lift, or your chest drops forward, when you squat deep, your ankles may not be bending enough to let your knees travel over your feet. Ankle mobility for deep squats is worth checking before you add depth or weight. Stiff ankles are not the only possible cause, so watch your squat first, then fix the right thing.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

## Why ankle mobility matters for deep squats

As you sink into a deep squat, your knees move forward and your shins tip over your feet. The ankle is the joint that allows that. When it runs out of range, your body finds the depth somewhere else: the heels come off the floor, the weight shifts onto your toes, or your chest folds forward to keep you balanced.

That is why two people can squat to the same depth and look completely different from the side. One stays flat-footed and upright. The other is on the balls of the feet with the back rounding, and adding a dumbbell to that version only loads the compensation.

Ankles are not the whole story, though. Tight hips, poor balance, a stance that is too narrow, or one side doing more work than the other can all change how a squat looks. One rep on its own will not tell you which.

## Heels lifting during squats: how to check yourself at home

You can get a fair first look with a phone and a mat. Prop the phone on the sofa at knee height, side-on to you. Stand barefoot on the mat, feet about shoulder-width, and squat slowly with no weight to the depth you can manage, five or six times.

Then watch the video. Look at whether your heels stay down all the way, whether your chest stays fairly upright or folds over your knees, and, once you film the other side, whether one heel comes up before the other.

That last one matters to me. A difference between left and right is the kind of muscle imbalance I want to understand before anyone does heavy strength work. If anything hurts during the check, stop. Mayo Clinic's advice is to stop an exercise that causes pain, and to try a lower weight or come back to it in a few days.

Treat this as a first look. It shows you what your squat looks like today. It does not tell you why, and it does not replace an assessment by someone who can test the joints one at a time.

## Ankle mobility exercises for desk workers: where to start

Start with the warm-up. The NHS says warming up helps prevent injury and makes workouts more effective, and suggests at least 6 minutes, including marching on the spot, heel digs and shallow knee bends where you lower no more than about 10 cm. Those last two get your ankles and knees moving before you ask them for a deep squat.

On top of that, I give every strength session about 10 minutes of mobility work, so the joints move well and the chance of injury drops. Which ankle drills go into those 10 minutes depends on what the assessment finds. I do not hand everyone the same five moves from a video, because a stiff ankle, a tight hip and a balance problem each need different work. More on why that order matters is in [mobility before heavy lifting](/blog/mobility-before-heavy-lifting/).

While you work on it, squat to the depth where your heels stay down, and build from there dheere dheere, a little at a time. Mayo Clinic says proper form and technique help you avoid injury, that a lighter weight makes form easier to control, and that anyone new to strength training should learn form from a trainer or other fitness specialist.

## When it is more than stiffness

If your ankle has a history, such as an old sprain or fracture, or swelling or pain that keeps coming back, treat it differently. The NHS says to get advice from a healthcare professional, such as your GP, before doing even its general warm-up exercises if you have an injury, a health problem or any symptoms, and to stop at once if you feel pain or become unwell.
