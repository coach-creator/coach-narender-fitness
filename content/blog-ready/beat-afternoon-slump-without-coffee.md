To beat afternoon slump without coffee, start with last night. The NHS says that if you regularly do not get enough sleep, you may feel very tired or sleepy during the day and find it hard to focus. That can be exactly what a 3 pm crash at a desk feels like. A fourth chai at 4 pm can then cut into tonight's sleep, and tomorrow's slump arrives on time.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Picture a Thursday. Dal-chawal at 1:30 pm, a long call at 2 with your camera off, and by 3 you are reading the same slide three times. Someone from the team is going down for chai and asks if you want one. You say yes, the same as yesterday.

## What a 3 pm energy crash at a desk job can mean

I cannot tell you why your energy drops at 3 pm without knowing more about you. What I can say is that sleep is the first thing I look at, because the official guidance links poor sleep so directly to how you feel during the day. The NHS says most healthy adults need around 7 to 9 hours a night. The CDC says adults aged 18 to 60 need 7 or more.

The number of hours is only half of it. The CDC describes quality sleep as uninterrupted and refreshing, and lists trouble falling asleep, waking up repeatedly in the night, and feeling sleepy even after enough sleep as signs of poor quality. Six hours of broken sleep after a late call with a US team is not six hours of quality sleep.

Stress comes into it. Many of the professionals I meet carry so much stress from work that they do not sleep properly. Food comes into it too. Body aapke work schedule ke hisaab se kaam nahi karti: your body does not run on your work schedule, and food is its fuel. If protein is low and the plate is mostly carbs, I would look at that as well. Read [protein for people who sit all day](/blog/protein-for-people-who-sit-all-day/) for that side of it.

## How too much office coffee affects sleep

The NHS lists tea, coffee, cola and energy drinks as caffeine drinks that can keep you awake. It recommends no tea or coffee for at least 6 hours before bed. The CDC goes further and advises avoiding caffeine in the afternoon or in the evening altogether.

Do the arithmetic for your own day. If you sleep at 11 pm, the NHS line puts your last cup at 5 pm, and the CDC advice would move it to the morning. The 4 pm chai that rescues the afternoon sits right on that line, and the 6 pm one with the evening meeting is well past it.

I am not asking you to give up chai. Move it earlier, have fewer, and watch what happens to your sleep over two weeks.

## What to do at 3 pm instead

Get up and move. I will not promise that a walk clears the fog, because none of the guidance I used says that. What the NHS does say is that if you are not active during the day, your body may not feel ready for sleep at night. It also advises avoiding exercise in the 4 hours before bed, and suggests the morning or early afternoon instead. A 3 pm walk around the floor or down to the building gate sits inside that window.

Walking is the part busy professionals ignore. Many think one gym hour means the day's work is done, and forget the hours of sitting around it. The NHS mentions 150 minutes of exercise a week as the recommended amount for adults, and a short walk in the workday is an easy place to start.

Your nights matter as much as your afternoons. The CDC suggests going to bed and getting up at the same time every day, keeping the bedroom quiet and cool, turning off devices at least 30 minutes before bed, and avoiding large meals and alcohol close to bedtime. Pick the one you break most often and fix that first.

## When tiredness needs a doctor, not another cup

Some tiredness is not about chai or bedtime. The CDC says to talk to a healthcare provider if you regularly have problems sleeping or notice signs of a sleep disorder, and names insomnia, restless legs syndrome, narcolepsy and sleep apnea among the common ones. The NHS says to see a GP if changing your sleep habits does not help or you think you may have insomnia. If you think a medicine is affecting your sleep, speak to a GP or pharmacist.

If you feel dizzy, faint or unwell rather than simply sleepy, that is a doctor's question first.

For five working days, note three things: when you slept, when you woke, and when you had your last chai or coffee. That short log will tell you more than another cup will.
