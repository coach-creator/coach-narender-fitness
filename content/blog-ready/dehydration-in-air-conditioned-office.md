Sitting in an air-conditioned office does not dehydrate you by itself, as far as the NHS and Mayo Clinic pages I checked are concerned. Neither names air conditioning as a cause. What they do describe is the ordinary reason dehydration in air conditioned office life creeps in: you are too busy to drink, and thirst is not always a reliable signal.

Say you reach the office at 9.30 with a bottle in your bag. Back-to-back calls, two cups of chai from the pantry, a working lunch. At 4 pm you notice a dull headache and feel flat. The bottle is still in your bag, cap sealed. Nothing in that cool meeting room ever reminded you to open it.

## Why dehydration in air conditioned office days is easy to miss

The NHS defines dehydration as your body losing more fluid than you take in. Mayo Clinic lists the simple causes first, and one of them reads like a desk worker's diary: not drinking enough because you are too busy to remember.

Mayo Clinic also notes that thirst is not always a good way to tell if the body needs water. Many people, mainly older adults, do not feel thirsty until they are already dehydrated. So waiting for thirst is a weak plan if your day is wall to wall.

A few things raise the risk further, according to the same two sources: drinking too much alcohol, sweating after exercise, hot weather, diabetes that is not controlled, and medicines that make you pee more, such as water pills and some blood pressure medicines. If you take any of these medicines, ask your doctor how much you should drink. That question belongs to them, not to me.

## Signs of mild dehydration at work

The NHS lists the symptoms of dehydration in adults. Most of them are easy to blame on a long day, which is why they get ignored:

- feeling thirsty
- a headache, or feeling light-headed or dizzy
- dark yellow, strong-smelling pee
- peeing less often than usual
- feeling tired
- a dry mouth, lips and tongue
- sunken eyes

The colour check is the most practical one for an office. You will pass the washroom several times a day anyway. If what you see is dark, that is your cue, whatever the room temperature.

Some signs need more than a glass of water. The NHS says to get urgent medical advice if you feel unusually tired, feel dizzy when you stand up and it does not go away, have dark pee or are peeing less than normal, or are breathing quickly or have a fast heart rate. Confusion, cold skin, unusual sleepiness, difficulty breathing, or pale, grey, blue or blotchy skin, lips or tongue are emergency signs. Get emergency help.

## How much water for desk workers in India

You will see neat litre targets all over the internet. The two sources I used do not give one for healthy adults, so I won't invent it. The NHS rule is simpler: drink fluids regularly through the day, enough that your pee stays pale. Water is a good choice, it says.

Then drink more when the risk goes up. The NHS names sweating from hot weather or exercise as one of those times. For anyone who walks to the car park in a Gurugram May afternoon, or trains in the evening after a full day in a cold office, that applies directly.

Mayo Clinic adds that fruits and vegetables hold a lot of water, and advises against drinks with caffeine or a lot of sugar, such as sodas and some energy drinks, because they can be dehydrating. The third chai is a habit worth noticing. If you have been vomiting or have diarrhoea, the NHS suggests asking a pharmacist about rehydration solutions. Leave the details to them.

## Why this matters for your workout

I often say your body does not run on your work schedule. Body aapke work schedule ke hisaab se kaam nahi karti. A packed calendar does not switch off its need for fuel and water.

That matters most in the evening. Say you go straight from a 6 pm meeting to a workout, having drunk little since lunch. Headache, light-headedness and tiredness are all on the NHS list, and you would rather find that out before the first set than halfway through it. If you feel dizzy or wiped out when you start training, read [what to do when you feel dizzy or exhausted starting a workout](/blog/dizzy-or-exhausted-when-starting-a-workout/), and get it checked if it keeps happening.

Sweat itself is only your body's temperature going up and your body cooling itself down. It tells you nothing about how good the session was. What it does tell you is that you have fluid to replace.
