To restart working out safely, start well below what you used to do, warm up every time, and build up slowly. Mayo Clinic's rule of thumb is to increase your activity by no more than 10% a week. If you have a health condition, an injury or symptoms, or you have been inactive and plan hard exercise, talk to a doctor before week one.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. ## Why people get hurt in the first weeks back

Mayo Clinic describes the pattern well. Many people start a fitness program with a lot of energy, then work out too long or too hard, and give up when their muscles and joints become sore or injured.

Say you used to squat with a loaded bar and run on weekends, before the promotion and two years of 7 am calls. The memory of that body is still in your head, so on day one you go back to the old weight. Your joints and your heart have had two years off. Week one has to start from the body you have today, not the numbers you remember.

## Check with a doctor first if this is you

The CDC says moderate activity is safe for most people. It also says to talk with your doctor about the right types and amounts if you have a chronic condition such as heart disease, arthritis or diabetes. Talk to your doctor before vigorous activity, it adds, if you have been inactive, have a disability or are overweight.

The NHS gives a similar list before its exercise videos. Get advice from a GP first if you have a health problem, an injury, any symptoms, are feeling unwell, or have had a recent health event such as a heart attack or an operation. The same goes if you are pregnant or have recently given birth.

## Measure where you are before you start

Mayo Clinic suggests writing down a few scores before you begin, so you have something to compare against later. Its list covers your pulse before and after a 1.6 km walk, how long that walk takes, how many push-ups you can do, how well your hips, knees, ankles, shoulders and elbows move, your waist measurement and your BMI. It suggests checking again six weeks in.

## Your first week back exercising after months off

Warm up every session. The NHS says a warm-up should take at least 6 minutes, longer if you feel you need it, and its page shows a simple routine you can follow at home.

For the aerobic part, Mayo Clinic suggests starting at a pace you can keep up for 5 to 10 minutes without getting overly tired, then adding time as your energy improves. It suggests working up to 30 to 60 minutes on most days. The CDC's weekly target is 150 minutes of moderate activity, which you can split into short sessions.

In my own plans, week one starts with basic mobility and simple movement, and it should feel easy. If you finish the first session thinking you could have done more, that is the right result.

## Returning to strength training after a break

Mayo Clinic says one set of each exercise is enough for health and fitness benefits, using a weight heavy enough to tire the muscle after about 12 to 15 repetitions. Train all the major muscle groups at least twice a week, and leave time between sessions to recover.

I would add my usual order. Do not jump straight into heavy strength work. First understand your muscle imbalances, put about 10 minutes of mobility before every session, and look after your heart and lungs. Then add load dheere dheere, slowly, inside that 10% a week.

## Stop signs

The NHS is direct: stop the exercise immediately if you feel any pain or become unwell. Mayo Clinic says that pain, shortness of breath, dizziness or nausea mean you should take a break, because you may be pushing too hard. It also says that a day or two off is fine when you are not feeling good.

If dizziness or exhaustion shows up in the first few sessions, read [feeling dizzy or exhausted when starting a workout](/blog/dizzy-or-exhausted-when-starting-a-workout/) before you push on.

## Make the restart stick

Mayo Clinic suggests scheduling exercise the way you would any other appointment. I tell clients the same thing: pick your days, book them in the calendar like a meeting, and keep them. Aap available raho, main aaunga. You stay available, and I will come to you.

If a full session does not fit, break it up. Both Mayo Clinic and the CDC say shorter sessions across the day still count, and any activity is better than none.
