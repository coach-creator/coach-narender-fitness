My answer on how to schedule workouts with a busy calendar is to choose fixed days and block them like a meeting. Then, before the week begins, decide where a missed session goes. Start with the number of sessions you can actually keep, even if that's one or two, and let short bits of movement fill the gaps.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

Picture a Tuesday. You planned a 7 am workout, then someone in another time zone moved a call to 7.30. The 6 pm review ran until 7.15, dinner was late, and the session didn't happen. On Wednesday the same thing repeats, and by Friday the week is gone without you ever deciding to skip it.

## Start with the sessions you can keep

When someone can't stay on a routine, I first look at what their routine really is: how much time goes to family, how much work the office is sending, and whether they're getting enough sleep. If three sessions a week aren't happening, I check whether one or two can.

Then I fix a time around your office hours. It doesn't need to be a full hour, and the first sessions start basic, with mobility. Body aapke work schedule ke hisaab se kaam nahi karti: your body doesn't run on your work schedule, so the schedule has to make room for the body.

## Block the days: how to schedule workouts with a busy calendar

Pick particular days and put them in the calendar the way you'd book a client meeting. When people train with me at home, my line for those days is simple.

> Aap available raho, main aaunga. You stay available, I will come.

The CDC says much the same in public-health language. Make activity a regular part of your daily or weekly schedule, write it on your calendar, and put a friend on both calendars if that helps. If time is the problem, watch one ordinary week and find at least five 30-minute slots. Write that week down and mark the slots you actually have.

Energy matters as much as minutes. Put the session at a time of day when you feel more energetic, and build up from a slow start. If you're flat by 9 pm after back-to-back calls, don't put the session there.

## What to do when a meeting cancels your morning workout

Decide the backup before you need it. With one person I train, a missed session moves to the evening, when I'm free too.

If the backup also falls through, do something short. The CDC's adult overview lets the weekly 150 minutes come in smaller chunks, and the WHO's 2020 guidelines removed the old requirement that aerobic activity come in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The WHO links activity of any duration to better health, so a brisk 15 minutes after the call counts.

## Use the small gaps in the day

Even a desk day has gaps. India's ICMR-NIN dietary guidelines list ways to stay active on a busy schedule: use a standing desk or stand every half hour, walk for 5 to 10 minutes every few hours, take phone calls while walking, use the stairs, park farther from work and move during TV breaks. The CDC adds walking meetings and standing or walking during calls.

Treat all of this as extra movement on top of the main weekly target. The WHO also says adults should limit sedentary time, and that replacing it with activity of any intensity, even light, has benefits. A walking call is a good place to start.

## What a week can look like

For adults, the WHO recommends at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening for all major muscle groups on two or more days. One CDC example week is a 30-minute brisk walk on five days and weight training on two other days.

If a founder has about 3 hours in the week, I split it across the week. A strength session is 30 to 40 minutes, with a 10-minute mobility warm-up and a short finisher, and one day is about 30 minutes of moderate cardio plus mobility. I go through those sessions in more detail in [strength training for busy professionals](/blog/strength-training-for-busy-professionals/).

## When "no time" is the real problem

Some weeks really are full. Still, many people I meet who say they have no time find time for Netflix, and won't give 15 minutes to their body until a problem becomes big. I say that gently, because stress and stacked projects break routines for good, hard-working people.

If the first sessions leave you dizzy or drained, that's a reason to stop and talk before anyone pushes harder. I look at sleep and food first. Dizziness is also on the list of things a doctor should see first, so mention it to yours before you push on.
