The way to stick to fitness advice is to stop trying to follow all of it. Pick the two or three things that matter most for your body right now, put them on your calendar like meetings, and start smaller than feels right. Of all the advice I give, clients usually apply about 20%. So the useful question is which 20% you choose.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

## Why you know what to do but cannot follow through

The people who come to me are usually smart and well educated: IIT and IIM graduates, successful businesspeople. They already know they should go easy on carbs and get enough protein. Even after being told, many still do not eat enough protein. Knowing has never been their problem.

What breaks the routine is usually the week itself. Stress gets so high that sleep suffers, projects pile up on top of each other, and staying regular becomes very hard. Say your Tuesday 7 am session gets eaten by a call with a client in another time zone. By Thursday the whole week feels lost.

There is also a quieter habit. People say they have no time, and then find an hour for Netflix at night. They will not give 15 minutes to the body, and wait until the problem becomes big before acting on it.

> "Netflix ke liye time hai, body ke liye 15 minute nahi." There is time for Netflix, but not 15 minutes for the body.

I do not say that to shame anyone. Body aapke work schedule ke hisaab se kaam nahi karti: your body does not run on your work schedule.

## How to stick to fitness advice: choose your 20% on purpose

If you will only act on a fifth of what you hear, pick that fifth deliberately instead of letting the easiest bits win. For most busy people over 35, my order is fairly steady. Mobility and heart and lung health come before heavy loads, joints and function come before looks, and food is fuel.

The baseline from the CDC is a useful frame: about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 days. The CDC also says some activity is better than none, and that the 150 minutes can be broken into smaller chunks across the week.

Another common miss is treating one hour of workout as the whole job, done for the day. Walking and everyday activity get ignored. If you choose only three things, a reasonable set is two strength sessions, more walking on ordinary days, and the protein you already know you are short of.

## Book it like a meeting

Mayo Clinic's advice is to schedule exercise the way you would any other appointment, and the CDC says to write it on your calendar. I say the same thing to clients, in fewer words. Pick fixed days, block them, and treat them like a meeting you would not cancel for a lesser one.

I take away the commute too. Aap available raho, main aaunga: you stay available, I will come. I train clients at their homes in Gurugram, so there is no commute and no gym bag, and the session happens where you already are.

If you do not know where the time could come from, the CDC suggests tracking your daily activities for one week and looking for at least five 30-minute slots you could use. One busy client of mine found the time by cutting late-night Netflix and low-value tasks.

## Simplest fitness habits for busy executives

Small habits stick because they fit inside a day you already have. The CDC suggests standing, stretching or walking during phone calls, holding walking meetings where you can, and taking the stairs. Say you have three back-to-back calls after lunch. Taking two of them on your feet, pacing the corridor, is movement you would not otherwise get.

When three sessions a week are not happening, I do not insist on three. First we see whether one or two can happen reliably, and build from there. A missed session can move to the evening instead of disappearing.

Timing matters too. The CDC suggests scheduling activity for the times of day when you feel most energetic. If you are flat by 8 pm every weekday, an evening slot is a plan built to fail.

## Start smaller than feels right

Mayo Clinic describes a common pattern. Many people start with a lot of energy, work out too long or too hard, then give up when muscles and joints get sore or injured. Its advice is to start slowly, build up gradually and plan rest between sessions.

Dheere dheere, slowly and steadily, is how I progress people. When a client has dizziness or blood pressure trouble early on, I look at sleep and food first before asking them to push harder. You can read more about [how I build a plan](/#method) around those basics.
