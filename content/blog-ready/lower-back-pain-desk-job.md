Lower back pain from a desk job is very common, and the NHS says back pain usually improves within a few weeks, often on its own. I'd start by staying active, changing position often while you sit, and walking more through the workday. If any warning sign below applies to you, see a doctor first.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. ## When should I see an orthopaedic doctor for back pain?

Warning signs come before any talk of training. The NHS says to call emergency services or go to A&E if you have any of these:

- pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs
- loss of feeling around the genitals or anus
- changes in the bladder or bowels, such as difficulty peeing, or peeing or pooing yourself
- a change in how the penis or vagina feels during sex, not being able to get or keep an erection, or not being able to orgasm
- chest pain
- back pain that started after a serious accident, such as a car accident

The NHS also wants an urgent appointment if you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell, or if severe pain starts suddenly or gets worse quickly. Mayo Clinic's list for immediate care is new bowel or bladder problems, a fever, and pain after a fall, a blow to the back or another injury.

Most back pain moves more slowly than that. Between them, Mayo Clinic and the NHS say to see a doctor if:

- the pain lasts longer than a few weeks, or a few weeks of home care hasn't helped
- it's severe and doesn't improve with rest, or it's worse at night
- it spreads down one or both legs, especially below the knee
- you have weakness, numbness or tingling in one or both legs
- you've lost weight without trying or can't explain it
- it stops your day-to-day activities, or you're worried or struggling to cope
- there's a lump or swelling, or your back has changed shape
- it's worse when you sneeze, cough or poo
- the pain is in the upper back, between the shoulders, rather than the lower back

## Does a desk job cause lower back pain?

Usually nobody can point to one cause. WHO says about 90% of low back pain is non-specific, which means no specific disease or structural reason can be found. The risk factors it names include low physical activity, smoking, obesity and high physical stress at work. Mayo Clinic adds age from about 30 or 40, stress, anxiety and depression, and lifting with the back instead of the legs.

Then there are the hours in the chair. The NHS says many adults sit for more than 7 hours a day, and that meeting the weekly activity target still leaves a risk if the rest of the day goes sitting down. That page is about heart disease, type 2 diabetes and weight gain, not back pain, but low activity is on the WHO list too. And the professionals I see after 35 or 40 often bring the same set: stiffness, lower back pain, knee or neck pain, and very tight muscles.

## How to sit with lower back pain

For lower back pain from a desk job, Mayo Clinic's sitting advice is short. Use a seat with lower-back support, armrests and a swivel base. Keep the curve of your lower back with a pillow or a rolled towel, keep knees and hips level, and change position often, at least every half hour. On a dining chair at home, start with the rolled towel.

Hold off on the special chair or back belt. Mayo Clinic says there's no good evidence that back supports or specially designed furniture prevent or relieve back pain. The ICMR guideline for Indians works on the hours instead: stand every half hour at work, walk for 5 to 10 minutes every few hours, take all phone calls while walking, and use the stairs instead of the lift.

Picture a day of calls with lunch at the desk. You take the calls that need no screen on your feet, and walk for a few minutes after lunch. That's the ICMR advice, and not one meeting had to move.

## What's safe to try while you keep working

The NHS says to stay active, keep up your daily activities and avoid long periods in bed. Exercises and stretches can help, but if one makes the pain worse, stop and see a doctor. Walking, swimming, yoga and pilates may ease back pain, the same page says.

WHO's self-care list goes wider than exercise: look after your mental well-being, keep a healthy weight, don't smoke, sleep well, stay involved in work and social life, and make ergonomic changes at work. It also says painkillers shouldn't be the first-line treatment for low back pain. Mayo Clinic's prevention advice is close to that: regular low-impact aerobic exercise such as walking, cycling or swimming, core strength and a healthy weight.
