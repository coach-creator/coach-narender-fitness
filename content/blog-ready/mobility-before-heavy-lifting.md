Mobility before heavy lifting is the order I use, and a busy week doesn't change it. I don't start anyone on heavy strength until I understand their muscle imbalance. About 5 to 10 minutes of mobility comes first, closer to 10 in a normal session, and heart and lung work sits in the same plan. A long held stretch is a separate job, and I keep it apart from the warm-up.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

## Should I do mobility before strength training?

The NHS says to warm up properly before exercising, both to prevent injury and to make the workout more effective. Its public routine takes at least 6 minutes, longer if you feel you need it. The page says its video series suits most people in good health with a reasonable level of fitness.

If that isn't you, the same page wants you to get advice from a GP or another health professional first. That covers being unsure the exercises suit you, a health problem, an injury, any symptoms, feeling unwell, or a recent heart attack or operation. And stop straight away if you feel pain or become unwell.

Stretching is a separate question. The American Heart Association quoted Dr Gary Liguori, senior editor of the American College of Sports Medicine's 2021 exercise guidelines, who said the evidence that skipping a stretch before a workout harms an average person is limited. His worry is people who never stretch at all, above all if they're sedentary or their week is one repeated activity. Someone who only walks, he said, won't gain shoulder or hip flexibility from the walking, and could lose range of motion even at 150 minutes a week.

The ACSM guidance he described is to stretch the major muscle groups at least two or three times a week, and daily is better. Most adults hold a stretch for 10 to 30 seconds, and older adults may hold up to a minute. Dr Kelley Gabriel, in the same article, says not to bounce, to move slowly inside the joint's current range, and to stop if it hurts. Don't twist back and forth quickly either. If you rotate at all, keep it slow.

A warm-up does a different job. Liguori describes it as an easier version of what you're about to do, which raises blood flow, and he says that rise may lower the risk of heart trouble from sudden hard effort. One reason people have heart attacks while shovelling snow, he said, is that they skip the warm-up. The same logic applies when you walk in from a day at the desk and reach for a heavy set.

## Mobility before heavy lifting after a day of sitting

Picture a Tuesday that ends in a 6 pm meeting. You get to a hotel gym, or back to the living room, and the hips and upper back feel set from the chair. The tempting move is one hard set, so the day counts as training.

I'd spend the first minutes on how you move instead. That's mobility before heavy lifting in practice: a 10 minute warm up for stiff muscles from sitting, before any heavy work starts. In founders and other desk professionals strength is a real gap, but what I see even more is weaker breathing and very tight muscles. Those minutes are there so joint mobility can improve and the chance of injury can come down.

Mayo Clinic's getting-started advice is gentle in the same way. Give yourself plenty of time to warm up and cool down with easy walking or gentle stretching, then find a pace you can keep for 5 to 10 minutes without getting overly tired. With an injury or a medical condition, it says to talk with a healthcare or fitness professional and build slowly, adding no more than 10% a week.

The NHS warm-up opens with marching on the spot and stays easy, and it's fine if you're in the group that page is written for. What I won't do is paste it, or any five-move list from a morning video, onto someone I haven't assessed. If you can't say what a movement is for, I leave it out.

If what bothers you is getting winded on stairs, that check comes before any hard conditioning block. I've written about it separately in [winded climbing stairs after 35](/blog/winded-climbing-stairs-after-35/).

## How to check muscle imbalances before lifting weights

The check is an assessment. I look at body type, mobility, disorders, medicines and mental health, and I ask what you did before, what you do now, how many days you can train and how motivated you are. Old injuries come up there. I use tests where they're needed, then we talk about a plan that fits the days you actually have, with a reason and a benefit for every exercise that you can repeat.

Only once the imbalance is understood does a heavy bar, a heavy dumbbell or the hard version of a bodyweight move come in. Joint health and ordinary function come before looks. People tell me they want about 5% of the fat gone so they look better, and they ask who needs to climb, or to run in an emergency. I won't trade the joints for that.

Heart and lungs stay in the same plan. A session that only chases a heavier lift, with no attention to breathing or to how you move, skips the part I see failing first in people who sit all day. Dheere dheere: the load goes up slowly, on purpose.

If something already hurts, a joint locks, or pain runs down the leg, the doctor comes before I load it. The full order of a first session is on [how I coach](/#method).
