Protein for people who sit all day doesn't need to be a big number. India's ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition sets the recommended amount at 0.83 g per kg of body weight a day for healthy adults, about 54 g for a 65 kg person. For most Indian desk workers, the gap lies elsewhere: protein quality, how it's spread across meals, and whether there's enough strength work for the body to use it.

## How much protein does a desk worker need in India?

ICMR-NIN gives two figures in its 2020 note on nutrient requirements: an average requirement of 0.66 g per kg a day, and a recommended allowance of 0.83 g per kg a day. Its 2024 Dietary Guidelines for Indians put those at about 43 g and 54 g a day for a 65 kg adult, regardless of physical activity or gender.

A cereal-heavy plate changes the maths. A footnote in the 2020 note says that for people on a cereal-based diet with low-quality protein, the requirement is 1 g per kg a day. For a 65 kg person living mostly on rice or roti, that comes to about 65 g.

The total is often not the weak spot, though. The 2024 guideline says an adult's daily diet often provides 60 to 70 g of protein, which is more than required. The problem it names is quality. Your body needs nine essential amino acids, and four of them are limiting in plant foods.

## Why protein matters for people who sit all day

Most of the professionals I train are smart, well-read people. Yet many of them eat a carb-heavy Indian diet, order food in, and still don't get enough protein, even after being told. Everyone knows to limit the carbs and at least raise the protein, and it still doesn't happen.

> Body aapke work schedule ke hisaab se kaam nahi karti.

Your body doesn't work according to your work schedule. If you're educated, you can understand how it works: food is the fuel, protein is part of that fuel, and you can set limits on how much of each you eat. After 40, a lot of problems start to show.

This is where the sitting comes in. The ICMR guideline says protein is unlikely to be used for building muscle without enough physical activity, and that muscle atrophy sets in when activity is low. Regular activity, including strength exercise, plus a good diet is what it calls important for keeping muscle. It also says protein without enough carbohydrate and fat energy isn't used efficiently for muscle, so I don't read any of this as a reason to cut rice to zero.

## High-protein Indian vegetarian lunch for the office

For vegetarians, ICMR's rule is simple: combine cereals and pulses at about 3 to 1 by raw weight to get all nine essential amino acids. Adding 250 ml of milk improves the quality further, and nuts and seeds belong in the mix too. On a plate, I see that as dal with rice, or rajma with roti.

Picture a working lunch: a big mound of rice, one small katori of thin dal, a little sabzi. As the rice grows and the dal stays small, the plate drifts away from that ratio. More dal, a bowl of curd, a glass of milk or a handful of nuts brings it back.

For variety, the guideline names the pulses to rotate: lentils, green gram, horse gram, black gram, chickpeas, kidney beans, cowpeas, soyabean and green peas. Its nuts and seeds include almonds, pistachios, cashews, walnuts, hazelnuts, chia, pumpkin, flax and sesame. For non-vegetarians, it mentions about 700 to 900 g a week of marine fish, poultry, lean meat or eggs.

If you like gram numbers, Harvard Health lists one egg at 6 g of protein, 6 ounces of plain yogurt at 17 g, and a cup of cooked beans at about 18 g. Those are American serving sizes, so treat them as a rough guide.

## Is 50 g of protein too much in one sitting?

Harvard Health says research suggests the body can make use of only 20 to 40 g of protein at one time. Its advice is to spread protein across meals rather than eating most of it at one large dinner.

If you skip breakfast, eat a rice-heavy lunch and have a heavy late dinner, that's the first thing I'd fix. Picture a day that starts with chai and biscuits, with lunch ordered in and dinner late. Adding milk, curd, eggs or a besan chilla in the morning moves some protein to the start of the day.

## Do you need protein powder?

ICMR is clear on this. Most athletes can meet the recommended amount from food, protein powders are not required, and intake above about 1.6 g per kg a day adds no further gains in muscle mass from resistance training. It also says prolonged intake of large amounts of protein powder, or a high-protein concentrate, has been associated with risks such as bone mineral loss and kidney damage.

My view matches. Supplements are for people who can't meet their protein from food, and only when there's no medical issue against them. If a doctor already follows a kidney problem or another condition, ask them before changing your protein.
