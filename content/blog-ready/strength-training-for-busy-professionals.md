Strength training for busy professionals can fit into about three hours a week. If a founder tells me that's all the time they have, I ask for 30 to 40 minutes of strength work per session, with 10 minutes of mobility first and a short finisher at the end. On one day, that session can become about 30 minutes of moderate cardio plus mobility. The rest of your weekly activity has to come from the workday itself, mostly walking.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

## What the guidelines ask of a busy adult

WHO, the CDC, the NHS and India's ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition all ask for muscle-strengthening work on at least two days a week. WHO pairs that with at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week for adults aged 18 to 64, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix of the two. The strength work should use all the major muscle groups, which the CDC lists plainly: legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms.

Two details help when your calendar is full. Strength work doesn't replace the 150 aerobic minutes, and the NHS says the two can sit on the same days or on different ones. WHO has also dropped its older rule that aerobic activity had to come in bouts of at least 10 minutes, because newer evidence links activity of any length with better health outcomes.

## How I split three hours of strength training for busy professionals

Most professionals I see after 35 or 40 come in with stiffness, a lower back or knee that complains, and stairs that leave them tired. With founders and desk professionals I also notice weaker breathing and very tight muscles. That's why the 10 minutes of mobility come first in every session, so the joints move better and the chance of injury is lower before any load goes on.

Then come 30 to 40 minutes of strength work and a short finisher. Some weeks all three sessions are strength days, and in others one of them turns into about 30 minutes of moderate cardio plus mobility. Across the week, the strength work should reach every muscle group on that CDC list.

For effort, the NHS has a simple test: do the strength work until you need a short rest before you repeat it. Its examples include weights, resistance bands, push-ups and carrying heavy shopping. Picture a hotel room on a work trip. A resistance band or a set of push-ups still matches those examples.

## Ask what each exercise is for

Which exercises fill those 30 to 40 minutes depends on the assessment. Before I choose them, I look at your mobility, your medical history and medicines, what activity you did before, and how many days you can really give. I won't hand you five exercises copied from a video, because the same list on every person ignores whatever is going on in that person's body.

Every exercise in your plan should come with a reason you could repeat back to me. If a coach can't tell you what a movement is for, ask again. ## Why heavy lifting waits

I don't put a new client straight into heavy strength work. First I want to understand the muscle imbalances, add those 10 minutes of mobility, and look after the heart and lungs. Then the load goes up dheere dheere, which means slowly, step by step. WHO says the same for anyone below the target: start with small amounts and raise frequency, intensity and duration over time.

Some of you should talk to a doctor before adding load. The NHS suggests a GP first if you haven't exercised for some time, or if you have a medical condition or a concern. The ICMR guideline says men and women over 40 should consult a doctor before a vigorous programme.

Many people come in wanting to look a little leaner and treat joint health as optional. Who needs to climb or run in an emergency anyway, they ask. I put joints and function first and let looks follow. Older people break bones slipping on stairs or a wet floor in the rains, and keeping muscle and heart health through the years is how you avoid that kind of needless injury.

## Is one gym hour enough if you sit all day?

A lot of people treat one hour of workout as the job done for the day, and ignore walking and everyday movement. WHO asks adults to limit sedentary time, and says replacing it with activity of any intensity, even light activity, has health benefits. People who sit a lot should aim above the recommended amount, and the NHS also says to break up long periods of sitting.

For an office day, the ICMR tips fit well: stand up every half hour, walk for 5 to 10 minutes every few hours, take all phone calls while walking, and use the stairs. The CDC lets the 150 minutes come in smaller chunks through the week, so those short walks still count toward it. Picture a Monday of back-to-back calls. The calls that need no screen can still happen on your feet.
