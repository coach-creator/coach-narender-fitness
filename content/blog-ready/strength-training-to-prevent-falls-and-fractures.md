Strength training to prevent falls and fractures does its best work as one part of a mix. The strongest evidence is for exercise that challenges your balance, and programmes that add resistance work to that look better still. Lifting on its own hasn't been shown to do the job, so I plan strength, balance and everyday movement together.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

## Why a fall costs more than a bruise

Barish ke din, geela floor: rainy days and wet floors. Older people slip on those, and on stairs, and a fracture is the injury I most want them to avoid.

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on falls puts numbers on it. It estimates about 842,000 deaths from falls worldwide in 2023, second only to road injuries among unintentional injury deaths, with about 71% of them in people aged 60 and older. Most falls aren't fatal, but they still cause fractures, head injuries, lost independence and long-term care. The injuries can also lead to less confidence, less activity and a cycle of increasing frailty.

The sheet also lists what raises the risk in older people: weaker balance, strength, gait, vision, thinking or mobility, and medicines or conditions that cause dizziness or low blood pressure. Some of that belongs with a doctor. Balance and strength can be trained, and that part is my job.

## What the evidence says on strength training to prevent falls and fractures

The biggest piece of evidence is a 2019 Cochrane review of exercise for preventing falls in older people living at home. It pooled 108 trials with 23,407 people in 25 countries, average age 76. Exercise of all types cut the rate of falls by 23% compared with a control not thought to reduce falls, which the authors rate high certainty. Balance and functional exercises cut it by 24%, also high certainty, and programmes that mixed several types, most often balance and functional work plus resistance exercise, probably cut it by 34%, with moderate certainty.

Two results are weaker. For programmes that were mainly resistance exercise, the authors say there wasn't enough evidence to judge what resistance training does without balance and functional work. On fractures, exercise may reduce the number of people who break a bone in a fall, but that result is low certainty, and the review says more studies are needed to confirm it.

WHO's advice points the same way. For older people living in the community, it recommends tailored exercise that challenges balance and builds functional strength, progressed regularly and kept up long enough, and it names Tai Chi as another option. So when I talk about strength training to prevent falls and fractures, I mean strength sitting next to balance and the movements you use every day. A heavy deadlift with no balance work doesn't match what was tested.

## Where bone comes in

Falls are one half. The other half is how easily a bone breaks when you land. Mayo Clinic explains that most people reach peak bone mass around age 30 and after that lose slightly more bone than they build, and that people who aren't physically active have a higher risk of osteoporosis than people who exercise regularly.

Weight-bearing exercise can help build strong bones and slow bone loss, and Mayo's examples are brisk walking, jogging, dancing, climbing stairs, and sports such as soccer, tennis and pickleball. That's a separate claim from the falls trials, and I keep the two apart. If you break a bone after 50, or you're worried about your bones, Mayo says to talk with a healthcare professional, because a bone density test may be needed.

## Why I start this in your 40s

Nothing on the pages above says muscle loss, or fall risk, begins at 40. The guidelines are written for people 65 and older. For that group, the CDC asks for muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week, covering legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms, along with aerobic activity and balance work such as walking heel-to-toe or standing from a sitting position.

The NHS asks the same age group for strength, balance and flexibility work on at least 2 days, and says this kind of work helps anyone who has fallen, or worries about falling, feel stronger and more confident on their feet. I'd rather start that work in your 40s, while the week is still full of meetings, than wait until a fall has already happened. That timing is my plan, not a finding in these papers.

## How I'd build it for you

It starts with an assessment. I look at body type, mobility, any disorders, medicines and mental health, what you did before and what you do now, and how many days you can actually train. Medicines are on that list because WHO counts the ones that cause dizziness among fall risks.

From there, the week has about 10 minutes of mobility before any heavy work, then strength for the major muscle groups, balance, and heart and lung work. Every exercise comes with its reason, so I won't paste the "5 best exercises" from a morning YouTube video onto you. The load goes up dheere dheere, slowly and on purpose.
