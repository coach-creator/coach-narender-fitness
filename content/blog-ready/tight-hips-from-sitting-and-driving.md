Tight hips from sitting and driving usually build up from long hours in one position, at the desk and then in the car. Harvard Health says hours of sitting tighten the hip flexors and hamstrings and stiffen the joints, even in people who are reasonably active, and that this may contribute to lower back pain. Moving more often through the day is the first step, and real pain needs a doctor before any stretch.

## When hip pain needs a doctor first

I start with the warning signs, because they decide whether we talk about training at all. The NHS says hip pain is sometimes serious, such as a fracture, and that you shouldn't try to diagnose the cause yourself. It says to call 999 or go to A&E for severe pain after a fall or injury, if you can't walk or put weight on the leg, or if there is tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg after an injury. Don't drive yourself there. In India, that means emergency care.

The NHS asks for an urgent GP appointment if severe pain starts suddenly without a fall or an injury to the hip, if the hip is hot and swollen, if the skin around it changes colour, or if the pain comes with feeling unwell and a high temperature (or feeling hot, cold or shivery).

A non-urgent GP visit is its advice if the pain stops your normal activities or sleep, keeps getting worse or keeps coming back, or hasn't improved after two weeks of home care. Hip stiffness that lasts more than 30 minutes after waking is on that list too.

If any of that fits you, see the doctor before you book a session with me. When imaging and an orthopaedic opinion are the right step, I send people for them and train with that guidance afterwards.

## Why you get tight hips from sitting and driving

Picture a Monday. Forty-five minutes in the car to the office, seven hours at the desk with the hips bent, then an hour in evening traffic on the way back. Your hips have spent most of the day folded at roughly the same angle, and they feel it when you stand up.

That's the pattern Harvard Health describes. Long hours of sitting, whether reading, using a computer or watching TV, tighten the hip flexors and hamstrings and stiffen the joints. Once they're tight, they can affect how you walk and balance, and may contribute to lower back pain and knee stiffness.

The back of the hips gets its share as well. In an article on what it calls "dead butt syndrome", Cleveland Clinic explains that prolonged sitting gradually weakens the gluteus medius, a muscle that steadies the hips and pelvis, and riding in a car counts as part of that sitting. The first signs it describes are numbness or tingling in the buttocks and awkward first steps after you stand. Over time, it says, this can grow into buttock, hip and lower back pain.

## Can tight hips cause lower back stiffness?

They can play a part, which is why I never look at the hips alone. Harvard Health says tight hip flexors and hamstrings may contribute to lower back pain, and Cleveland Clinic's stretch note runs the other way: if the lower back is tight, the hip flexors are often tight too. ## Hip mobility for desk workers and drivers

For tight hips from sitting and driving, the first change is to sit less in one stretch. Harvard Health suggests breaking up long blocks of sitting with a timer to get up every so often, standing for phone calls, and trying a standing desk. Cleveland Clinic goes further, with at least 20 minutes of standing or walking in every hour and an alarm to get up and stretch roughly every half-hour.

The car is the harder half. The NHS advises people with sore hips not to sit in low chairs, because they put extra pressure on the hips. Neither the NHS page nor Harvard's mentions driving, but on a long drive I apply that line myself: raise a low seat if you can, and stop to stand when it's safe.

The Cleveland Clinic article also covers sitting posture and a set of glute and hip-flexor exercises, with reps and hold times, and I'll leave that detail to the page.
