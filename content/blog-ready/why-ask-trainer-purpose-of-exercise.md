Ask your trainer the purpose of every exercise because the answer tells you whether the session was built for your body or copied from somewhere. A good answer names what the movement does and why you need it now, for your stiff hips, your weak back or the knee that complains on stairs. If the answer is vague, ask again until it is not.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

Say you are 41, a trainer comes to your home in Gurugram three mornings a week, and today is lunges, burpees and a plank. You do all of it and you are tired by 7:30. On the 9 am call, you could not tell anyone why those three were chosen.

## Why ask your trainer the purpose of an exercise at all

My opinion is simple. Do not just do what the trainer says. Keep asking what this exercise is for, and why it is in your plan. You should come to understand your own body through what your trainer explains, and that only happens if you ask.

Many people see it the other way. The trainer has been assigned, the trainer will change my body, and whatever the trainer says, I will do. That hands over the one thing you should keep, which is knowing what is being done to your body and why.

When I train someone, I explain the reason for each exercise and what it should do for them. Clients have written in their reviews that I explain the science behind every move, and that is the standard I would hold any trainer to.

## What a trainer should know before choosing your exercises

The purpose of an exercise comes from information about you. Before I write a plan, I assess the body: mobility, any disorders, medicines, mental health, what you did before and what you do now, how many days you can actually train, how motivated you are, and a few specific tests. Without that, the trainer is guessing.

The official advice points the same way. The NHS says to speak to your GP first if you have not exercised for some time or have medical conditions or concerns, and to make sure the activity and its intensity suit your fitness. The Mayo Clinic says that anyone with a chronic condition or safety concerns should talk to a healthcare professional before starting. When an X-ray, an MRI or an orthopaedic opinion is the right step, I send people for it and then train with the doctor's advice.

## How to tell if your personal trainer knows anatomy

You cannot quiz a trainer on muscle names in the middle of a session, and you do not need to. Listen to how they answer the questions below. Several of the good answers come straight from the Mayo Clinic's strength training guidance, so you can check them yourself.

- What is this exercise for, in my body specifically?
- Why this one, and not another that works the same area?
- Where should I feel it, and what do I do if it hurts? (Mayo's advice is to stop an exercise that causes pain, then try a lower weight or come back to it in a few days.)
- How will we know when to make it heavier? (Mayo suggests increasing the weight gradually once you can easily do more repetitions.)
- Which days do these muscles rest? (Mayo recommends one full day of rest between sessions for the same muscle group.)

A trainer who knows the body will connect each answer to you: your desk hours, your old injury, the goal you gave them. Exercises also come from different kinds of training, such as rehab, yoga, sports, bodybuilding, powerlifting and weightlifting. A plan that mixes them without a reason, or gives everyone the same pattern, is a warning sign. I will not call every trainer wrong, and many do pay attention to these things. But I have seen trainers on a team watch a "five best exercises" video on YouTube in the morning and give the same pattern to every client that day, with no medical history taken. The industry is unorganised, so nobody checks. You have to.

## Training as a class vs workout entertainment

> Aap class le rahe ho, entertainment ke liye nahi aa rahe.

You are taking a class. You are not there to be entertained. Many people find the basic work boring and want the session to feel exciting, so some trainers fill the hour with jumping, CrossFit-style circuits and constant movement. Thaka ke bhej dete hain: they tire you out and send you home. Sweating feels like proof, but sweat only means your body temperature went up and the body is cooling itself down. I wrote more on that in [does sweating mean a good workout](/blog/does-sweating-mean-a-good-workout/).

The Mayo Clinic stresses proper form and technique to help avoid injuries, and suggests that anyone new to strength training work with a trainer or fitness specialist to learn correct form. That is slow, careful work, and it is supposed to be.

Pick one exercise in your next session and ask what it is for, and why you are doing it today. If the answer makes sense for your body, good.
