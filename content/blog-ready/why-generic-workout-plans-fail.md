Ask me why generic workout plans fail and my answer is short: they're written before anyone has asked about you. A list of "5 best exercises" doesn't know your knees, your back, the medicines you take, how active you were last year or how many days you can really train. A plan has to start with those questions, and every exercise in it needs a reason you can say back to me.

This is general education from a personal trainer, not medical advice. Back pain, chest pain, dizziness, a joint that locks, pain down the leg, or a condition a doctor already follows needs a doctor first. Narender sends clients for imaging and an orthopaedic opinion when that is the right step, then trains with the doctor's guidance.

Picture a Sunday night. You save a 30-minute video called the best home workout, and on Monday you do it in the living room before a 9 am call. The jump squats feel rough on a knee that already complains on the office stairs, but the video keeps going, so you keep going. By Thursday you can't tell whether the plan is helping, hurting or doing nothing, because nothing in it was chosen for you.

## Why generic workout plans fail before the first set

I've seen where those lists come from. When I worked on a gym team, some trainers would open YouTube in the morning, look up five exercises someone had called the best, and give every client that day the same pattern. They had watched plenty of videos. Nobody had taken a medical history.

Sabko galat nahi kahunga, I won't call every trainer wrong, and many do pay attention to a client's history. Still, a lot of people in this work never studied it. They liked training, built a good body and started coaching, and because the industry is unorganised, nobody checks.

The exercises themselves come from different jobs, too. Some belong to yoga and rehab, some to sport, some to general fitness, and others to bodybuilding, powerlifting or weightlifting. Mix them without a plan and you can end up with a lifter's drill in a week that was meant to settle a stiff back.

## What the screening guidelines look at first

Doctors have their own version of these questions. In 2015 the American College of Sports Medicine updated its advice on health screening before exercise. The paper's abstract says there is considerable evidence that exercise is safe for most people, and that exercise-related cardiovascular events are often preceded by warning signs or symptoms. Its screening rests on how active you are now, whether you have signs or symptoms or known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, and how hard you plan to train.

A video knows none of that about you. The same abstract also says the older rules can result in too many referrals to a doctor and possibly create a barrier to exercise. The newer advice tries to find people who may be at elevated risk of exercise-related sudden cardiac death or a heart attack, while reducing unnecessary barriers for everyone else.

The NHS, the CDC and India's ICMR-NIN guidelines say the everyday version in plainer words. If you haven't exercised for some time, or have a medical condition or concern, speak to a GP first. If you live with a chronic condition such as heart disease, arthritis or diabetes, ask a doctor which types and amounts of activity suit you. Moderate-effort activity is safe for most people. Match the intensity to your fitness, and raise intensity and duration gradually.

## What medical history should a trainer take?

Before I write anything, I look at body type, mobility issues, any disorder, the medicines you take and your mental health. I want to know what physical activity you did in the past and what you do now, how many days a week you can actually train, and how motivated you are. Some specific tests come after that, and only then do we discuss what the plan can include around your availability.

Sometimes the history or the tests point somewhere a trainer shouldn't go alone. Then I send the person for an X-ray or MRI and an orthopaedic opinion, and I train with the doctor's guidance. A video in your saved list can't make that call.

## How should a personal trainer customise a workout?

The plan is built around one person: which muscle groups need work, how flexible they are, their heart health and the days they really have. I don't start anyone on heavy strength. First I want to understand the muscle imbalance, keep around 10 minutes of mobility in the session and look after the heart and lungs. I explain that order in [mobility before heavy lifting](/blog/mobility-before-heavy-lifting/). Joint health and function come first, and looks follow.

Then there's the why. Ask your trainer what an exercise is for, and why it's in your session today, and expect an answer in plain words. A tired body tells you very little about whether the work suited you.

## If you still want to follow a free plan

Free plans can be useful once you know what you need. The CDC's advice for sticking with activity is to choose things you enjoy that match your ability, and it offers a planner and a diary rather than one fixed routine. For a sanity check, the NHS targets are strength work for all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week, plus at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity.

Build up slowly, and take the rest signs seriously. ICMR-NIN lists shortness of breath, pain, nausea, vomiting and headache as signs the body needs rest. If any of those show up, or a joint keeps hurting after the same exercise, stop and get it checked before you press play on the next video.
